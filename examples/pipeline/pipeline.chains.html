Two Ways to Break a Chain

independentChains and multipleAccumulators both split a serial dependency into parallel lanes, both are legal, and both land within a hundredth of the same speedup. The dependency graph is where they differ — one is four lanes deep, the other is eight lanes wide, and the shape is what decides how many lanes you need.

Example 1: four lanes, each still four deep

dependentChain multiplies one accumulator by four derived factors per element, and each multiply reads what the one above it just wrote. The critical path is the whole loop: four multiplies × 100M elements, with nothing available to fill the stall slots.

The fix gives each of four accumulators its own copy of that work, striding i += 4. Each lane keeps its full four-multiply depth — what changes is that four of them now sit side by side with no edges between them, and the four partial products multiply back together at the end.

Example 2: eight lanes, each one op deep

singleAccumulator looks similar in prose — one running value, a dependency every iteration — but its graph has a different shape. There is no four-op recurrence here. Each element contributes exactly one add, so the chain is N long and one deep.

The two shapes, at one scale

Strip the labels and the difference is a rectangle versus a strip. This is the whole distinction between the two examples, and it is the thing that decides how many lanes each one needs.

Both splits are legal — and the compiler proves it

Follow the recombination at the bottom of each graph. Example 1 multiplies its four partial products together; Example 2 adds its eight partial sums. In both cases the recombining operator is the same one the lanes used, and in both cases that operator is associative — which is exactly the property that makes splitting the chain a transformation rather than a rewrite. Both programs print their verdict when you run them.

legal · a product

independentChains

The whole loop is one big product, so four lanes of partial products multiply back together exactly. Unsigned arithmetic keeps the wraparound well-defined; the factors are forced odd so repeated even multiplies can't drive the accumulator to zero.

Same answer? YES

legal · a sum

multipleAccumulators

Eight partial sums over disjoint strides, recombined by addition. Because integer + is associative and commutative, the fold is exact and the transformation preserves the result for every input.

Same answer? YES

The strongest evidence isn't the printed verdict — it's what happens at -O2. Both gaps close, because the compiler performs both splits itself. Dumping the assembly for dependentChain shows it directly: a strict four-deep chain at -O1, four independent partial products at -O2. 

; -O1 — each mul reads the destination of the one above
mul x11, x0,  x10
mul x11, x11, x12
mul x11, x11, x12
mul x0,  x11, x10

; -O2 — four independent partial products, compiler-generated
mul x10, x10, x14
mul x11, x11, x15
mul x12, x12, x16
mul x13, x13, x17

An optimizer is only permitted to do that if the result is unchanged. When the compiler takes your hand-optimization away, that is the proof it was valid — and conversely, a split the compiler refuses to perform for you is often one that wouldn't have preserved the answer.

what this example used to get wrong

Example 1 originally built its four-deep chain out of +, *, ^ and >> mixed together. That stalls just as convincingly, but the mix is not associative — so the four lanes recombined to a completely different value (86345932972 against the serial 1531458671438922123). It was never a transformation of the baseline at all, just a different program with a similar cost profile. Switching the chain to a pure product kept the stall and made the split real.
